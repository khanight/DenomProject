# Worldbuilding

## The Four Factions

The world is divided among different races and four magical factions: **Craft**, **Void**, **Life**, and **Death**. Each faction has its own distinct magic system, and each has a defined stance toward the other three. Race and faction are separate axes — see Peoples & Soul Ancestry, below.

The factions form a diamond with a single axis of open rivalry and no favoritism otherwise:

- **Life** and **Death** — direct opposites; open dislike
- **Craft** and **Void** — direct opposites; the other axis of the diamond
- Beyond that one rivalry axis, there's no built-in ally relationship: every faction tolerates the two factions it isn't directly opposed to equally, with no structural favoritism toward either one.

So each faction has one outright rival (directly across the diamond) and two factions it merely tolerates — evenhandedly, not one favored over the other.

The animosity between opposite factions isn't just doctrinal — it's structural. Soul-linking (see Partnered Casting, below) is forbidden outright between practitioners of opposite factions, so Life and Death mages, or Craft and Void mages, can never bond on a soul level with each other at all. That enforced lack of understanding is what curdles into open dislike rather than mere rivalry.

### Dual Aptitude: Opposed Pairs — No Precedent

Because soul-linking is forbidden between opposite factions, the system has no institutional or legal pathway for a person who shows genuine aptitude in both halves of an opposed pair — Life+Death or Craft+Void. There's no protocol for training, classifying, or claiming such a case — which makes a confirmed dual aptitude dangerous to reveal openly: neither faction has a framework to absorb it peacefully, so whoever discovers it has to choose between surfacing it and burying it. Discovery is usually fatal, too: known opposed-pair duals tend to come from a bloodline that once merged taboo magic — a past Life+Death resurrection or Craft+Void fold-forging worked somewhere in the family line — and that inherited taint is reason enough on its own for the bearer to be killed on discovery, not just silenced.

The opposite-faction soul-link ban cuts both ways for a dual carrier: since their soul carries both halves of an opposed pair, linking with a single-affinity partner from *either* of their own two factions would itself be the forbidden opposite-faction link, just routed through one persona instead of two. That locks a Life+Death dual out of partnering with Life or Death mages entirely — their only safe partners are Craft or Void mages, whose factions merely tolerate rather than oppose either half of them. The same logic runs in reverse for a Craft+Void dual, who can only safely partner with Life or Death mages. (This is exactly why the protagonist's two personas both train and partner within Craft sub-factions, and why his Craft+Void-dual partner, the sea-faction love interest, is the one caster whose combined ability with his own isn't structurally forbidden — see characters.md.)

Two known instances exist in the story so far: the protagonist (Life+Death) and his sea sub-faction partner/love interest (Craft+Void) — each concealing their aptitude from the other as well as from the world (see characters.md).

### Dual Aptitude: Adjacent Pairs — Revered

Dual aptitude across any non-opposed pair (Craft+Life, Craft+Death, Void+Death, or Void+Life) is the mirror case. Soul-linking isn't restricted between these factions, so there's no structural barrier the way there is for opposed pairs, and such casters are admired rather than feared: still bonded to one partner at a time like any caster (see Partnered Casting, below), but bringing two magic systems' worth of options into that bond instead of one.

### Faction Cultures

- **Death — vibrant and loud.** Death's culture centers on festival, color, and music: the dead are celebrated openly and loudly, not mourned in silence. Death lives underground, in huge cavern systems — the festival culture unfolds in vast subterranean halls rather than under open sky.
- **Life — authoritarian regime.** Life presents itself as the most orderly, harmonious faction, but that order is a facade over a rigid dictatorship — a secret agenda the rest of the world hasn't uncovered yet. Life dwells on the surface, in forest cities built high into giant magic trees — sprawling treehouse metropolises whose orderly, tiered construction mirrors the regime's outward image of harmony.
- **Craft — nomadic wandering castles.** Craft strongholds are mobile, castle-like settlements that roam in search of raw materials, matching Craft's hands-on, material-focused magic (see specialties below). These castles can transform to cross land or sea as needed, and Craft splits into two nomadic sub-factions — land and sea — each with its own real-world cultural touchstone, trading with each other as much as with outside factions. The sky isn't Craft's domain — that airspace belongs to Void's floating islands (see below) — though a flying-Craft variant isn't ruled out for later.
  - **Land sub-faction** draws on a Mongolic/Central-Asian-flavored, multi-clan steppe-nomad culture: mobile ger-camp-style settlements, horse-and-caravan logistics, and master smiths who hold near-sacred status, all bound together by an oral, honor-bound guild-and-clan culture rather than a state. Unusually for Craft, the land sub-faction is deliberately open — it takes in non-magic people who want to learn a non-magic craftsman's trade, training them alongside its magical smiths on parallel, mundane trade paths.
  - **Sea sub-faction** draws on a Polynesian/Austronesian wayfinding culture: star-and-current navigation and voyaging-canoe fleets are the seed for castles that cross open water, and a navigator's status there rivals a chief's.
- **Void — nomadic and secret.** Void mages are nomadic too, but in the opposite register from Craft: rather than visible wandering castles, they move covertly, teleporting from place to place. Their home bases are floating islands adrift in the sky — the one domain Craft's castles don't share — linked to the ground by fixed, static portals used for regular travel to and from an island. Individual mages also carry a limited personal teleport ability of their own, letting them jump straight back to their home island without a portal in a pinch. Culturally, Void reads as a Tibetan/Himalayan-inspired monastic order: hidden monasteries perched in cloud and mountain, oracles, and esoteric knowledge passed on only to sworn initiates. That gives Void's information economy a contemplative, doctrine-guarding flavor rather than a mercenary one — what's being kept is closer to guarded teaching than street gossip, traded through reputation and favor rather than sold outright. Membership is correspondingly exclusive: Void draws almost entirely from those born into it and only rarely initiates outsiders from Life or Death — a sharp contrast with the land Craft sub-faction's open door (see above). This makes Void the faction best positioned to learn what's really happening inside Life's regime, long before anyone else does — a natural, built-in way for the plot to surface Life's secret agenda. (See Void Magic: Specialties, below, for how individual Void mages train.)

### Faction Law: Regulation of Magic

The four factions don't govern the use of magic the same way, and the sharpest contrast is between the two opposed on the Life/Death axis:

- **Life — licensed and tightly regulated.** Casting Life magic without permission and a proper license is banned outright, and in practice licenses and positions of power go only to wood elves (see Peoples & Soul Ancestry, below). Practice is gated by the state, which fits the regime's rigid, orderly facade (see Faction Cultures, above) and gives it a built-in lever of control: whoever issues the licenses decides who may heal, who may soulforge, and who may not. Unlicensed practice is a crime in itself, however benign the working.
- **Death — permissive, governed by moral rules.** Death is the mirror image: everyone is encouraged to perform magic as often as they like, with no licensing gate, in keeping with the open, celebratory festival culture. The limits are a short set of key rules grounded in moral standing rather than permission:
  - Never cause cruelty to an animal soul or a human soul.
  - Binding an *unwilling* soul is officially banned.
- **The gap between law and practice.** The unwilling-soul ban is real on paper but not in practice: unwilling binding is still performed, and the unwilling-soul wing (see Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship, below) is where it lives. Death's honor-system approach has no licensing body to catch it, so enforcement rests on community norms alone, which quietly leaves room for the ban to be ignored. This also complicates the "loving and kind" image Death presents: the faction's stated ethics and its actual conduct don't fully match.

(Craft's and Void's own regulatory stances are unset — see notes.md.)

### Craft Magic: Specialties

Craft magic isn't a single discipline, but Craft trains its mages to the opposite extreme from Void (see Void Magic: Specialties, below): a Craft mage commits to exactly one of the specialties below and takes it to its absolute limit, with no secondary or utility specialty alongside it. That one specialty is what defines what kind of Craft mage a character is:

- **Magic Blacksmithing** — weapons and armor aren't shaped from raw ore so much as "grown" through heat and hammering; the forging process itself is the spell, so a blade's power comes from the rhythm and ritual of the smithing, not just its materials.
- **Turret/Siege Constructs** — animated stationary artillery (ballistae, cannons, arc-lightning turrets) that, once "keyed" to their maker, target and fire autonomously — metal guard-dogs rather than weapons someone has to operate.
- **Base-Building/Fortification Magic** — walls, bastions, and traps raised directly from raw material on demand, letting a Craft party build a defensible camp mid-battle instead of carrying supplies. This is a core reason Craft armies wage war differently than Life or Death armies do.
- **Golem/Automaton Crafting** — full humanoid or beast-shaped constructs animated by a Craft mage's will. Unlike turrets, golems are mobile and can be taught simple behaviors, which raises the open question of how close a golem can get to personhood.
- **Runesmithing/Enchanting** — a subtler specialty: inscribing passive, always-on effects onto existing objects (armor, doors, tools) rather than creating something new. Fits a character who empowers others' gear instead of fighting directly.

### Void Magic: Specialties

Void trains its mages at the opposite end of the spectrum from Craft, and to a different degree than Life or Death. Craft mages commit to a single specialty pushed to its absolute limit, with nothing else beside it (see Craft Magic: Specialties, above). Life and Death mages sit in the middle of the spectrum: each takes one or two primary specialties — their "wing" (Shaper/Mender for Life, willing/unwilling for Death; see below) — plus one or two minor utility specialties alongside it. Void inverts the whole spectrum: it treats even competency across *all* of its disciplines as the harder, more prestigious skill than depth in just one or two. A Void mage who spikes hard into a single discipline and neglects the rest is a rare, faintly eccentric figure in this faction — not the norm the way specialization is everywhere else.

The disciplines a well-rounded Void mage trains across:

- **Portalsmith** — building and maintaining the fixed, static portals that link floating islands to the ground. Slower and far more effortful than a personal jump, but correspondingly more powerful and stable — infrastructure-scale Void magic, as opposed to an individual mage's quick escape-hatch teleport.
- **Rift-Warden** — opens a scrying-window onto another location or dimension without physically sending anything through it. Pure reconnaissance, and a natural extension of Void's secrets-and-favors economy (see Faction Cultures, above): a way to generate intelligence without committing to a full teleport or summon.
- **Blinkblade** — chains rapid, short-range micro-teleports in melee rather than committing to one long jump, flickering in and out of an opponent's guard. The one discipline most likely to produce an actual specialist despite Void's balance ethos, since it rewards being pushed hard in isolation the way a frontline fighter's kit does in other factions.
- **Mindseer** — reads surface thoughts, secrets, or probable futures at range. The magical expression of the same instinct that drives Void's social trade in secrets, rather than a separate skillset bolted on beside it.
- **Pocket-space binding** — everyday utility magic: folding small pockets of "elsewhere-space" into bags, pouches, or wagons. This is how a covert, portal-hopping culture handles logistics without needing Craft's wandering castles to haul goods.

### Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship

Death magic doesn't kill or rot anything — it manages souls in transit, guiding, binding, and releasing them. This is a deliberate subversion of necromancy-as-horror: Death mages aren't grave-robbers or ghouls, they're loving and kind, and the faction's loud, colorful festival culture (see Faction Cultures, above) is the visible expression of that care — a proper, celebratory send-off for the dead rather than mourning in silence.

- **Choice at death.** A soul isn't dragged anywhere against its will by default — it chooses for itself whether to remain in the world of the living or to cease to exist. Either way, a soul cannot act on or interact with the physical world on its own; it needs Death magic to do that. This is what makes Death mages necessary rather than incidental to the dead — souls can't "solo it."
- **Willing souls have requests.** A soul that willingly stays behind almost always does so because it wants something — unfinished business it communicates directly to its Death mage via telepathy. Once that goal is achieved, the soul generally passes on of its own accord. This gives individual souls concrete motivations and makes them a ready source of character options and side-plot hooks (see notes.md — Open Questions).
- **Animal souls.** Not every bound soul is human. Animal souls are simpler in nature than human souls, but no less powerful. Rather than voicing a specific unfinished-business request the way a willing human soul does (see above), an animal soul wants its binder to act in keeping with its nature — a bear soul wants to be paired with a tank, an owl soul wants stealth, and so on. It's a druidic influence on how the bound pair fights, not literal shapeshifting, and Partnered Casting's no-solo rule still applies in full (see Partnered Casting, above). Binding one cross-faction extends the existing combos: paired with Void, an animal soul can be bound into a Void-summoned creature of a matching body type (see Void + Death, above); paired with Craft, it can be bound into an automaton, a suit of armor, or a weapon, alongside the existing Craft+Death vessel-binding described below.
- **Self-binding.** A Death mage doesn't need a Craft partner or a vessel object to bind a soul at all — a soul (human or animal) can be joined directly to the mage's own living body. Despite the name, this isn't really a binding in the same sense as Life+Death or Craft+Death soul-binding (see Cross-Faction Magic, above) — nothing persists in a vessel once the working ends, because there's no vessel, only a live channel between mage and soul. It's a temporary joining: the soul lends the mage its power and wisdom for as long as the chant is sustained, and the moment the chant finishes or the soul is physically separated from the mage's body, the benefit ends outright — there's no lingering charge to draw on afterward. That fragility is the tradeoff for not needing a Craft partner or object. Binding an unwilling soul this way is very dangerous (nature of the risk still undecided — see notes.md, Open Questions).
- **Soul-binding (Craft + Death).** Souls, willing or unwilling, can be bound into objects — weapons, armor, or even whole buildings — through the Craft+Death cross-faction combo (see Cross-Faction Magic, above). A willing soul makes for a far better-functioning binding than an unwilling one, though the raw strength of the soul itself matters too. Binding an unwilling soul is officially banned under Death's moral rules, yet still done in practice (see Faction Law: Regulation of Magic, above). The cost is real: a bound soul cannot pass on to peace for as long as it remains bound. That cost has a hard ceiling, though: a binding also releases automatically the moment the Death mage who cast it dies. Since a mage can only bind souls during their own working lifetime, no bound soul can realistically stay trapped longer than a human lifespan — roughly 90 years at the outside.
- **The two specialist wings.** Death mages split along this same willing/unwilling line — those who specialize in working with willing souls, and those who specialize in working with unwilling ones. It's the faction's primary internal divide, closer to two schools of practice than a rivalry, and it's a Death mage's primary specialty; most also pick up one or two minor utility specialties alongside it (see Void Magic: Specialties, above, for how this fits the four-faction specialization spectrum). (The protagonist's Death persona, trained in the sea Craft sub-faction, specializes in willing souls — see characters.md.)
- **Fixed strength.** A soul already in Death's care can begin very strong — but it doesn't grow stronger from there; its power is fixed for the rest of its existence. This is the inverse of a newly soulforged Life soul, which begins weak and grows (see Life Magic: Soulforging, below).
- **Soul-boosting (Death + Death).** Paired Death mages can draw the same way on souls already bound in their care, strengthening themselves and allies fighting alongside them — the Death-side mirror of Life's soulforged soul-boost (see Life Magic: Soulforging, below).
- **Soul-damage.** Some Death mages — drawn from the unwilling-soul wing — can work the binding process in reverse: rather than guiding or holding a soul, they damage it directly, and because a person's soul and body are linked, that damage carries straight through to the body. It's the dark mirror of a Life Mender's soul-healing (see Life Magic: Soulforging, below): where a Mender heals the soul to heal the body, this wounds the soul to wound the body.
- **Fading from memory.** A soul that lingers for centuries or millennia loses its personality once no one living remembers it — memory is what keeps a soul's identity intact over time. This is why Death's festival culture matters so much practically, not just emotionally: the living carry the wills of the dead, Día de los Muertos-style, and remembering the dead out loud is part of what keeps them who they were. It's also why truly ancient souls are rare: once everyone a soul ever knew has died, there's no one left to protect, help, or take revenge for — the goals that motivate a soul to linger (see "Willing souls have requests," above) run out at the same time its personality starts fading from disuse, and both push it toward the same choice to finally move on.
- **Deathsong.** Soul magic is performed through song and dance rather than plain gesture — Death mages are performers as much as spellcasters, which fits the faction's festival culture and makes for a discipline a competition crowd can actually watch (see Magic Schools & the Cross-Faction Competition, below).

### Life Magic: Soulforging

Where Death magic guides, binds, and releases souls that already exist, Life magic can *originate* one — coalescing a brand-new soul out of raw vital energy. It's Life's own "playing god" axis, distinct from (and no less taboo than) the Life+Death resurrection combo (see Cross-Faction Magic, above): Death manages souls, Life creates them.

- **Weak start, earned growth.** A newly soulforged soul begins very weak — the inverse of a soul already in Death's care, which can begin very strong but stays fixed at that strength for good (see Fixed Strength, above). A soulforged soul's fragility cuts the other way: with sustained nurturing and encouragement from its creator or a caregiver, it can grow stronger over time. Left untended, it destabilizes and fizzles out within days or weeks — soul-creation is an ongoing responsibility, not a one-off cast.
- **The two specialist wings.** Life mages split into **Shapers**, who originate new souls, and **Menders**, who sustain and heal existing life — mirroring Death's willing/unwilling divide (see The Two Specialist Wings, above). This is a Life mage's primary specialty; most also pick up one or two minor utility specialties alongside it (see Void Magic: Specialties, above, for how this fits the four-faction specialization spectrum). A Mender's healing works soul-first: it heals a person's soul directly, and the body's healing follows from that, rather than the other way around. Some Death mages can work the same link in reverse, damaging a soul to damage the body (see Soul-Damage, above).
- **Full creation (Life + Life).** A lone Shaper can only forge the soul itself. Producing a complete new living body to carry it — in animal form — takes two Life mages working together: Life+Life partnered casting creates a soul and a physical body as one working.
- **Soul-boosting (Life + Life).** Paired Life mages can also draw on souls they've already soulforged to strengthen living souls — not just their own, but those of allies fighting alongside them. The Death-side mirror of this is Death+Death soul-boosting (see Fixed Strength, below).
- **Lifesong.** Life magic's performative counterpart to Deathsong (see above) is worked through cultivation ritual — planting, pruning, tending — rather than song and dance, with the rite reaching into and moving growing things as it's worked.

### Partnered Casting

**Core fundamental:** No magic can be cast solo, period — not cross-faction, not even within a single faction. Every casting requires an active partner. There's no known way around this, which closes off any solo shortcut: a caster with aptitude in two opposed factions (see Dual Aptitude: No Precedent) still cannot combine them alone, since the partner requirement applies regardless of whether the partner shares their faction or not.

The quality of any cast — same-faction or cross-faction — isn't fixed by raw skill alone; it scales with how well the two casters work together: their teamwork, how well they understand each other, and their ability to improvise together, especially under combat pressure. A strong partnership produces a materially stronger effect than two skilled-but-unfamiliar casters attempting the same combo.

### Partnered Casting: The Mental Link

Casting partners are telepathically linked for the duration of a working — able to literally hear each other's thoughts, not just coordinate through word or gesture. Underneath that, the link is a soul-bond: partners are briefly bonding their souls together to cast at all, which is part of why the requirement can't be routed around with an unwilling or indifferent stand-in.

- **Proximity vs. bond strength.** Physical closeness makes the link easier to hold, but a strong bond substitutes for it: the better-practiced and closer two partners are, the farther apart they can range and stay linked. Legendary partnerships can reportedly hold the link at distances up to a kilometer.
- **Attention is the maintenance cost.** The link doesn't run passively — each partner has to be actively thinking of the other to keep it up, and it weakens if attention drifts. This makes focus itself a combat vulnerability: a distracted or overwhelmed partner is a partner whose link is fraying.
- **A built-in channel for dialogue.** Because the link carries real thought, not just tactical signal, it doubles as an in-combat conversation — platonic banter between friend-partners, or, in the protagonist and his sea partner's case, an unusually intimate channel for a relationship neither of them can otherwise afford to have anyone see.

**Cross-faction magic** is the special case of this rule where the required partner belongs to a different faction. When practitioners from two different factions work together, their magics can combine into effects neither could produce alone:

- **Void + Life** — pulls living things across dimensions: creatures most often, but also flora, terrain fragments, or localized weather and hazards — a summoned storm or a patch of alien plant life with plot-relevant properties, not always a monster
- **Life + Craft** — grow living plant structures (architecture, tools, or defenses made of living plant matter); can also bind a newly soulforged soul into a crafted object — armor, a weapon, or other items — the Life-soul counterpart to Craft+Death's vessel-binding (see Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship, below). A Mender's soul-healing (see Life Magic: Soulforging, below) can be worked the same way into crafted medical equipment — bandages, splints, tools — letting the object carry the healing so it can be used without the Mender's hands-on touch.
- **Void + Death** — their joint magic is the most doctrinal of the combos: pulling souls through dimensional rifts to bind them as servitors, or erasing a body and soul into the Void so permanently that Life magic can never raise it again. The latter serves as a canonical funerary rite shared between the two factions.
- **Life + Death** — direct opposites; the taboo combination, and categorically different from every other way of binding a soul (see Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship, below): where Void+Death and Craft+Death bind a soul into a non-living vessel — a rift-servitor, a crafted object — Life+Death binds a soul into a living body, which makes it, at root, resurrection. The same decay-and-growth clash can also be turned on living tissue without a soul involved, producing undying-but-rotting flesh constructs or cursed crops that feed people while slowly killing them — but soul-into-living-body is the combination's defining, taboo act.
- **Craft + Death** — Craft grows or forges the vessel (weapon, armor, or even a building) while Death magic draws a soul, willing or unwilling, into it (see Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship, below). The Craft side supplies the object; the Death side supplies the binding. The same partnership also lets a soul-damaging Death mage (see Soul-Damage, below) channel that ability into a Craft-made weapon or poison — arrows, blades, toxins — that wound the soul, and so the body, on contact, rather than requiring the Death mage to touch the target directly.

- **Craft + Void — Fold-forging.** Craft shapes an object while Void folds space into it: bags-of-holding, self-assembling portable structures, or weapons that strike from a "folded" angle — an attack that visually arrives from nowhere. At true institutional scale, this is also how the floating islands and their static portal network were built in the first place — a historical joint Craft+Void engineering effort, not a solo invention. That history is exactly why the combo is now forbidden outside sanctioned, large-scale works: a botched fold doesn't just fizzle, it collapses inward and detonates as a black hole capable of leveling entire countries, and the risk doesn't shrink with the ambition of the attempt — even a "small" fold carries the same failure mode. That puts Fold-forging alongside Life+Death resurrection and unwilling self-binding as one of the system's true taboos: power with no margin for error, and no partner-based check strong enough to make it safe.

**A pairing that spans all four.** Partnered Casting requires an active partner regardless of aptitude, so a solo dual-aptitude caster still can't combine their own two affinities alone (see Dual Aptitude: No Precedent, above). But two dual-aptitude casters covering the *opposite* axes — one Life+Death, one Craft+Void — can, as a single partnered pair, between them reach all four factions' magic and every defined cross-faction combo above, with the undefined Craft+Void and Craft+Death combos potentially now in reach too. This is the case for the protagonist and his sea sub-faction partner (see characters.md).

### Partnered Casting: Group Combat

Once more than one casting pair fights together, partners aren't locked to each other for the whole fight — they can switch mid-combat, recombining into new pairs on the fly as the situation demands. This layers group fights into two levels that matter roughly equally: **micro** (how well the two currently paired casters work together in the moment) and **macro** (the whole party's coordination of who switches to whom, and when). Strong macro play can cover for a weaker micro pairing, and vice versa — making party-wide teamwork as decisive as any individual partnership.

## Peoples & Soul Ancestry

### Race and Faction Are Separate Axes

Faction is a magical allegiance; race is a people. Each faction contains several races, and each race can be spread across several factions. Magical aptitude isn't bound to a people, which is consistent with the protagonist's neutral, non-magic parentage (see characters.md).

**Humans** are the baseline: the ordinary, non-magic majority that populates the neutral factions, on land and in the oceans (see notes.md — Open Questions). They are the concrete home of the protagonist's origin, and the contrast case against the magical peoples.

### How Each Faction Relates to Race

- **Life — a strict racial hierarchy.** Life's cities take in many races, but only **wood elves** are permitted to practice Life magic and hold positions of power. Everyone else lives under them, unlicensed and shut out of authority. This is the concrete mechanism behind Life's licensing regime (see Faction Law, above) and a natural fault line under the regime's facade of harmony. Wood elves are also Life magic's race of origin, and the strict breeding rules they enforce are why it stays almost entirely confined to them (see Faction Origins & Marriage Customs, below).
- **Death — open to all.** Every race with a Death affinity may practice the magic. Death magic has spread far beyond its race of origin (still undecided) because the faction marries for love rather than by rule (see Faction Origins & Marriage Customs, below).
- **Void — sky elves.** Void draws almost entirely from **sky elves** (also called void elves — the originating race of Void magic): lanky, tall, skinny, and pale, and able to move quietly. That fits a faction of born-in members and covert, portal-hopping nomads, and it means the rare outside initiate from Life or Death (see Faction Cultures, above) would stand out physically as well as culturally.
- **Craft — the hub of multiculturalism.** Craft's cities and castles, on land and at sea, are where the world's races mix most freely. The land sub-faction's open door to non-magic tradespeople (see Faction Cultures, above) adds to this. Craft magic's race of origin is undecided, but it has spread to nearly every people because Craft admits and pairs by skill alone (see Faction Origins & Marriage Customs, below).

### Faction Origins & Marriage Customs

Each faction's magic originates in one particular race. How far it has spread beyond that race comes down to who its marriage customs allow into the family line, which, given that aptitude is inherited through the soul (see Soul Ancestry, below), decides who can pass the magic on.

| Faction | Race of origin | Marriage rule | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Life** | Wood elves | Strict breeding rules, enforced by the wood elves themselves | Almost none; mostly limited to wood elves |
| **Void** | Void (sky) elves | Strict rules on who may enter the culture; once a race has been admitted, it is a suitable marriage partner for every Void member | Small; mostly sky elves by default, plus the few races that have gained entry |
| **Craft** | Undetermined | Pure skill decides partners: a meritocracy with equal opportunity for all | Nearly every race |
| **Death** | Undetermined | Marry for love | Far and wide |

- **Life — closed by breeding.** The same wood-elf monopoly on licenses and power (see Faction Law, above) is reinforced at the bloodline level: the strict breeding rules keep Life magic from leaking into other peoples' lines.
- **Void — closed by admission, open once admitted.** Void controls who may enter its culture, so it stays overwhelmingly sky elves. The exception is any race that has entered: those peoples count as suitable marriage partners for all of Void, not just for some houses. This sits alongside Void's practice of picking marriages to balance disciplines (see Soul Ancestry, below).
- **Craft — meritocracy, and pathways for the non-magical.** Skill alone decides who partners with whom, which is why Craft magic has spread to almost every race. The system also provides pathways for a non-magic master of a craft to partner with a magic master of the equivalent craft — a non-magic blacksmith might marry a magic blacksmith. This extends the land sub-faction's open door to non-magic tradespeople (see Faction Cultures, above), and it fits the world's view that everyone has a soul, magical or not, so a skilled non-magic partner is a full match rather than a lesser one. (Same-class specialist pairings are still common, per Soul Ancestry below.)
- **Death — spread by love.** Partners are chosen for love with no gatekeeping, so Death magic has traveled through many peoples.

### Soul Ancestry

This world has no DNA-style ancestry. A person's soul is literally a combination of their parents' souls and their lines of descent, and that soul is what is inherited.

- **Aptitude is inherited through the soul.** If a child's parents spent their lives specializing in one discipline, the child has a natural affinity for it. Animal bonding works the same way: a lineage with long animal-soul partnerships passes that affinity down. This is a matter of natural affinity only and does not add literal shapeshifting (see Death Magic: Soul-Stewardship, above, on animal souls).
- **Each faction breeds by its own philosophy:**
  - **Void** deliberately picks marriages to balance out each family's tendencies toward particular disciplines, in service of its ideal of true balance across all of them (see Void Magic: Specialties, above).
  - **Craft** tends to marry specialists of the same class to reach even greater fluency, and passes those skills on to their children (see Craft Magic: Specialties, above).
  - **Life** prizes nobility and heritage bloodlines, which means the wood elves.
  - **Death** picks partners purely for love.
- **Dual aptitude.** This gives the "bloodline" behind opposed-pair dual aptitude (see Dual Aptitude: Opposed Pairs, above) a concrete mechanism: the taint that a past taboo working left on a family line is carried in the soul.

### Magic Schools & the Cross-Faction Competition

Each faction runs its own magic school to train its practitioners. Craft, being split into land and sea nomadic sub-factions, doesn't have one unified school — each runs its own.

Across a training cycle, all these schools build toward a cross-faction magic competition: an inter-faction tournament where students are ultimately tested against practitioners from other schools and factions, which puts the Partnered Casting rule (see above) front and center — a student's showing reflects as much on their partner and their chemistry as on individual skill.
